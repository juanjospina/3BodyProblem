Fermi paradox · cosmic sociology

The Dark Forest

Liu Cixin's answer to one of science's great open questions: if the universe is so big and so old, why is it so quiet?

1The Fermi Paradox

In 1950, physicist Enrico Fermi asked over lunch: But where is everybody? Our galaxy has 100–400 billion stars and is over 13 billion years old. Most stars have planets. Life on Earth appeared almost as soon as it could. Even a slow civilization could cross the galaxy in a few million years. Yet we see no ships, hear no signals. The Great Silence.

Play with the Drake equation, a rough way to estimate how many civilizations should be out there. Every number is a guess; scientists disagree by many orders of magnitude.

Bright dots: civilizations broadcasting. Faint dots: civilizations that exist but stay silent.

Possible answers to the paradox:

Rare Earth

Complex life needs so many lucky accidents that we might be nearly alone.

The Great Filter

Some step (life, intelligence, or surviving one's own technology) is almost impossible. It may still be ahead of us.

Too far, too brief

Civilizations are separated by vast distances and times, and their signals fade before anyone hears them.

The Zoo

They know we're here and are deliberately leaving us alone.

★ The Dark Forest

The universe is full of life, and all of it is hiding. Any civilization that reveals itself gets destroyed. Silence is how you survive.

Liu Cixin · The Dark Forest

2Cosmic sociology: two axioms

At Yang Dong's grave, Ye Wenjie gives Luo Ji the seed of a new science, a sociology of civilizations:

Axiom 1

Survival is the primary need of civilization.

Axiom 2

Civilization continuously grows and expands, but the total matter in the universe remains constant.

In other words, resources are limited. Every civilization wants to keep growing, but the universe isn't getting any bigger. Sooner or later, growth means taking space, energy and matter that someone else also needs. Civilizations are natural competitors, even if none of them is evil.

Concept: Chains of suspicion

You can never know whether another civilization is benevolent, or whether it believes you are.

Concept: Technological explosion

Technology can leap forward suddenly. A primitive civilization today can be a threat tomorrow.

Add one fact, that space is enormous and messages take years to cross it, and Luo Ji derives everything else.

3Chains of suspicion

Between people, suspicion can be broken by talking, sharing culture, and reading faces. Between two civilizations light-years apart, with completely alien minds, it can't. Each extra layer of “but what do they think I think?” costs another round trip at light speed.

4Technological explosion

When Trisolaris heard from Earth in 1979, humans were far behind. But the fleet needs about 450 years to arrive. In just the last few centuries, human technology went from muskets to spaceflight. By arrival, who would be stronger? This is exactly why the Trisolarans sent sophons ahead to freeze Earth's basic science.

5The logic: strike first

You have just discovered another civilization. You can't know whether they're benevolent (chains of suspicion), and even if they're weak now, they may not stay weak (technological explosion).

Only striking first guarantees survival (Axiom 1). Everyone can reason this out, so everyone hides, and everyone shoots at whatever shows itself. That is the dark forest:

“The universe is a dark forest. Every civilization is an armed hunter stalking through the trees like a ghost… If he finds other life, there's only one thing he can do: open fire and eliminate them.”

The invaders

Trisolaris

A world with three suns, the real mathematics behind it, and the civilization and technology that came out of it.

1The three-body problem

Two bodies (a sun and a planet) are perfectly predictable: Newton solved it in 1687, and orbits are clean ellipses forever. Add a third body and there is no general formula. Tiny differences grow exponentially, so the system is chaotic. That is the Trisolarans' nightmare: their planet orbits three suns, so they can never predict tomorrow's sky.

❄ frozenhabitableburning 🔥

1687 · Newton

Solves two bodies exactly. The Sun–Earth–Moon problem reportedly “made his head ache”.

1767–1772 · Euler & Lagrange

Find special exact solutions: three bodies in a line, or at the corners of a rotating triangle.

1890 · Poincaré

Proves there is no general closed-form solution and, in doing so, discovers chaos.

1912 · Sundman

Finds an infinite series that technically solves it, but it would need around 108,000,000 terms to be useful.

1993–2000 · The figure-8

Moore discovers, and Chenciner & Montgomery prove, a beautiful stable dance of three equal masses. Computers have since found thousands more periodic orbits.

Real Alpha Centauri

4.2–4.4 light-years away. Stars A and B orbit each other every ~80 years. Proxima is far out, on an orbit of ~550,000 years. In reality it is fairly stable; the novel exaggerates. Proxima b, a real planet, was found in 2016.

2Life under three suns

Stable Eras

The planet orbits one sun steadily for years or centuries. Civilization blooms.

Chaotic Eras

Suns rise at random. Long freezing nights, or days that burn. When three suns rise together, a “tri-solar day”, the planet is scorched.

Flying stars

When the suns are far away they look like small stars, a warning of deep cold.

Dehydration

Trisolarans can dry out into lifeless fibrous rolls, be stored through a Chaotic Era, and be rehydrated when a Stable Era returns. “Dehydrate!”

Hundreds of restarts

The Three Body game numbers its civilizations past 200. Each one is destroyed by the suns and rebuilds from scratch.

Transparent minds

Trisolarans communicate by displaying their thoughts, so they cannot lie. Deception is alien and terrifying to them, which is why the Wallfacers' hidden plans are humanity's weapon.

A totalitarian society

Survival above all. The individual is worthless and emotion is a weakness. Listener 1379's pity is a crime.

That is the Trisolarans' motive: their world is a death trap, and Ye Wenjie handed them the address of a planet with one calm sun.

4Sophons: unfolding a proton

To freeze Earth's science, Trisolaris turns a single proton into a supercomputer. A proton has 11 dimensions, most curled up tiny. Unfold some and it becomes enormous.

On Earth, sophons spy on everything, sabotage particle accelerators so physics experiments return nonsense, project images onto retinas (Wang Miao's countdown), and talk to Trisolaris instantly through quantum entanglement.
